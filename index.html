Pre-Brief

Read before you play. The debrief assumes you understand these. ~6 minutes.

+The scenario: Meridian Aviation Fuels, FY26

You're a regional fuel supplier serving a major hub airport. The chain has four serial nodes and moves a single grade — Jet A-1, measured in thousand-gallon lots (kgal). Each simulated month you (a) receive whatever shipment arrived, (b) fill the order from your downstream customer as far as inventory allows, and (c) place a replenishment order upstream.

The catch is structural, not personal: orders take time to travel upstream, and fuel takes time to ship back down. By the time you react to a demand signal, it's already stale. This is the engine of the bullwhip effect, and no amount of individual cleverness fully escapes it — but good policy dramatically reduces the damage.

You will not be told the demand curve or the disruptions in advance — just like real operations. Demand shifts with the seasons, and shocks arrive without warning. Each run is different. React to what you see, keep your orders smooth, and we'll reveal the full year in the debrief.

+How a month resolves (the sequence)
  • 1. Receive: the shipment that left your supplier L months ago arrives into your inventory.
  • 2. Demand arrives: your downstream customer's order (or, for Into-Plane, true airline demand) lands.
  • 3. Ship & fill: you ship min(inventory, demand+backlog). Anything unfilled becomes backlog — a promise you still owe.
  • 4. Order: you decide how much to order from upstream. This is your only lever.
  • 5. Costs accrue: holding cost on leftover inventory, penalty cost on backlog.

Lead times here are compressed to months so a full fiscal year fits in one sitting; in reality these are days-to-weeks, but the dynamics are identical.

+The dashboard — what each metric means

Modern fuel logistics teams track resilience and reliability, not just cost. Your live dashboard mirrors 2025-era best practice:

FILL RATE
% of demand shipped on time from stock. The cleanest read on supply adequacy. World-class ≥ 98%.
OTIF
On-Time-In-Full: share of months delivered complete with zero backlog. Partial = fail.
DAYS OF SUPPLY
Inventory ÷ recent demand. Your buffer against the next shock. Too low = fragile; too high = cash trapped.
BULLWHIP RATio
Variance of your orders ÷ variance of your demand. Above 1.0 means you are amplifying. The whole game in one number.
TTD / TTR
Time-to-Detect and Time-to-Respond to a disruption — resilience "clock speed." Faster beats bigger buffers.
TOTAL COST
Holding + backlog penalties, cumulative. The scoreboard the CFO sees.
+The four operational causes of the bullwhip (Lee et al.)
  • Demand-signal processing: treating each customer order as a forecast and padding it with safety stock.
  • Order batching: waiting to place large, infrequent orders instead of smooth, frequent ones.
  • Price/promotion fluctuation: forward-buying when fuel is cheap, distorting true demand.
  • Rationing & shortage gaming: over-ordering during shortages to win allocation, then cancelling.

Watch which of these you fall into when the disruptions hit.

+What earns a good grade

Lowest total cost is not the only goal — a chain can be cheap and dangerously fragile. You're scored on a blend of cost efficiency, fill-rate reliability, and bullwhip discipline, with a resilience bonus for detecting and absorbing shocks quickly. Aim to keep orders smooth, hold a sensible buffer, and resist panic-ordering when an event hits.
